Fueling for long training sessions adds up fast. An energy gel runs $1–2 each and you go through several on a long ride or race; a box of premium drink mix is $40–50. After looking at the ingredient labels on the commercial stuff, I noticed the main ingredients are just two sugars — maltodextrin and fructose — so I started buying the raw powders and mixing my own.

The result: it's cheaper, and I get full control over the carb ratio, sodium, and flavor. A 90g-carb bottle costs me roughly $1.70 to mix, versus $3–4 for the equivalent in a bottled mix.

Below I'll walk through the science, the recipe, electrolytes, and the cost, one section at a time.

---

## Why two sugars: glucose and fructose

First, why you can't just dump in any sugar.

Your body has a ceiling on how fast it can absorb carbohydrate during exercise. Glucose (including the glucose that maltodextrin breaks down into) is absorbed in the small intestine via the SGLT1 transporter, which saturates at about 60g per hour. Past that, extra glucose just sits in your gut and causes trouble, no matter how much you drink.

Fructose takes a different road — the GLUT5 transporter. It doesn't compete with the glucose pathway, so taking both sugars together lets the total absorption rate stack up. This is the idea behind "multiple transportable carbohydrates." Studies show that glucose plus fructose in a 2:1 ratio raises carbohydrate oxidation by roughly 50% over glucose alone, getting close to 90–108 g/hr<sup>[[1]](#ref-1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref-2)</sup>.

Maltodextrin is the glucose source here. It's a glucose polymer (a chain of glucose units) that breaks down to glucose in the gut. Using maltodextrin instead of plain glucose powder has a bonus: for the same amount of carbohydrate, a polymer produces far fewer osmotically active particles than single sugars (glucose, fructose), so you can pack more carbs into a bottle without it getting too concentrated, too sweet, or too hard on the stomach.

## Two ratios: 2:1 and 1:0.8

Once you get the principle, the rest is just the ratio. Maltodextrin breaks down almost entirely to glucose, so the "maltodextrin : fructose" ratio by weight is roughly the "glucose : fructose" ratio.

I use two ratios, switching based on how much carbohydrate I'm taking per hour:

| Carbs per hour | Maltodextrin : Fructose | When |
|---|---|---|
| **60–90 g/hr** | **2:1** | General long sessions, half-distance (70.3), moderate-intensity rides |
| **90 g/hr and up** | **1:0.8** | Race day, full-distance (140.6), long high-intensity efforts needing high carb |

These aren't numbers I made up. Early work by Jeukendrup's group established 2:1 as the most efficient combination around 90 g/hr<sup>[[1]](#ref-1)</sup>. Later research at higher intakes (90–120 g/hr) found that shifting the ratio closer to 1:0.8 pushes exogenous carbohydrate oxidation a little higher<sup>[[3]](#ref-3)</sup>. In short: at lower intake, 2:1 is plenty; only when you push past 90 g/hr do you need to raise the fructose share to 1:0.8.

Worth noting: there's no single "best ratio." The optimal one shifts with how much you actually take in. So don't memorize a number — just remember the direction: the more you take, the higher the fructose share.

In actual grams, per bottle:

| Carbs per bottle | Maltodextrin | Fructose | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60g (2:1) | 40g | 20g | 2:1 |
| 90g (1:0.8) | 50g | 40g | 1:0.8 (about 1.25:1) |

## Don't forget electrolytes

A carb-only drink is incomplete. Long sweaty efforts lose a lot of sodium, and drinking plain water without replacing it can actually dilute your blood sodium.

The general guideline is 500–1000mg of sodium per hour, adjusted for your sweat rate and the weather<sup>[[4]](#ref-4)</sup>. The cheapest way to add it is table salt (sodium chloride): salt is about 39% sodium, so 1g of salt is roughly 390mg of sodium. That works out to 1.3–2.5g of salt per bottle to cover an hour.

If you want to dial it in more precisely, get a sweat sodium test. I did the Precision Fuel & Hydration test and came back at 1331 mg/L, which is on the high side. But concentration alone isn't enough — the sodium you actually need per hour equals "sweat sodium concentration × sweat rate," so you also have to estimate how much you sweat in an hour. If you sweat a lot and salty like I do, you'll want to be at the top of the 500–1000mg range, or higher.

If you don't like the salty taste, you can swap part of the salt for sodium citrate, which is gentler on the palate — and it's what a lot of commercial products use too.

## Flavor: fruit powder, citrus, and the Pocari problem

Plain maltodextrin and fructose taste pretty flat — maltodextrin is nearly flavorless and fructose is just sweet, which gets cloying after a while. Plenty of options to fix that:

- **Fruit powder**: I bought a few fruit-flavor powders (passionfruit, peach, lemon, blueberry) — a small amount gives it a juice-like taste, and it's cheap. This is my go-to. One caveat: the powder itself also contains maltodextrin, glucose, and fructose, so strictly speaking it's an estimate too. But the amount is tiny — about 10g per liter is plenty of flavor — so it barely moves the overall 2:1 or 1:0.8. Just ballpark it.
- **Lemon juice / citric acid**: a squeeze of lemon or a little citric acid adds a clean tartness and balances the sweetness, and it doesn't affect the carb ratio at all.
- **Mixing in a sports drink**: I still use Pocari Sweat as a base sometimes — it tastes better than what I mix myself.

But using Pocari as a base has a catch: **the amount of sugar it adds is a different order of magnitude than fruit powder.** Pocari is made from sucrose plus high-fructose corn syrup plus glucose, with 6.6g of carbs per 100ml<sup>[[5]](#ref-5)</sup>, and it doesn't list how much is glucose versus fructose. Fruit powder adds a few grams — minor. Pocari as a base dumps in tens of grams of sugar at an unknown ratio, which throws off the 2:1 or 1:0.8 you carefully dialed in.

So my rule is by session: for shorter, lower-intensity workouts where I'm only taking ~60 g/hr, I'll flavor with Pocari — at that intake I'm nowhere near the absorption ceiling, so the ratio drifting a bit doesn't matter much and tasting good wins. But for high-carb training, or precise race fueling, I keep an unknown-ratio sugar like Pocari from being a big share, and own the main carbs myself.

## How I actually mix it

It's simple. A few things that matter:

- **Weigh it, don't scoop it**: maltodextrin and fructose have different densities, so measuring by volume is way off. Get a cheap kitchen scale and work in grams.
- **Mix fresh — it's quick**: I don't batch a big jar in advance. For outdoor rides I weigh the powder into the empty bottle the night before, then buy water at the start and shake; for indoor rides I mix right before I start. It takes almost no time.
- **Concentration depends on the goal**: research generally suggests 6–8% (30–40g of carbs per 500ml), which is close to isotonic and absorbs best. But for high-carb training I deliberately mix it stronger — at that point the drink is hypertonic, so carry extra plain water alongside.
- **Train your gut**: you can't drink 90 g/hr on the first try. Gut absorption is trainable, so start adapting in training so race-day intake isn't a surprise.

## Caveats

- **This isn't medical or nutrition advice**: gut tolerance varies a lot between people. Start low, test it in training, and never try a new mix for the first time on race day.
- **High concentration goes hypertonic**: past ~90g of carbs the drink gets concentrated, and without enough water it can upset your stomach.
- **Hygiene**: keep your dry powder sealed and dry, and don't let mixed drink sit in the heat for long.
- **Commercial products still have their place**: I've used Maurten and Precision Fuel & Hydration too, and honestly they taste better than what I mix — plus the hydrogel tech, ready-made electrolytes and flavors, and the tear-and-go convenience on race day are things you can't replicate at home. They just cost a lot more. My split: use them for races and race-prep fueling, mix my own for everyday training.
- **I haven't ditched commercial gels either**: in training, especially run sessions, I still carry gels. A powder needs a bottle and you have to drink it on the move, which is a pain running; a gel goes in a pocket and you're off. So: self-mixed drink for the bike and long sessions, gels for the run.

---

## Wrap-up

The core of a commercial sports drink is just maltodextrin plus fructose — the trick is sending glucose and fructose down separate gut pathways to stack the absorption rate. Get the principle ("2:1 for 60–90 g/hr, 1:0.8 above 90 g/hr"), add enough sodium and a bit of flavor, and you can mix a bottle that costs a fraction of the commercial stuff — and that you control completely.

If you train a lot and your fueling bill is starting to sting, it's worth a shot. Start on training days, get the recipe and your gut dialed in, then take it to races.
